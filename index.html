Session 6.3 · Saturday · Self-paced

Writing and Reading Clinical Rules

This week you learned what a decision support system is and how its rules are written down. Here you write one yourself — slot by slot, then as a decision tree, then as a set of alerts you have to triage into tiers.

Informatics in Pharmacy Practice · 1213404 Maps to sessions 6.1 and 6.2 Learning outcome S1 Approximately 97 minutes
Before you start

No prior programming experience is required, and none is expected. If you can write an if-then sentence you can read every line in this module. What is being tested is clinical reasoning expressed precisely, not code.

A

Warm-up: what you must already know

8 min

Five statements from this week that carry disproportionate examination weight. Read each, then cover it and reconstruct it before moving on.

Session 6.1 · Classification

Rules you can read, or patterns learned from data

Knowledge-based systems apply explicit rules and can be read and audited. Non-knowledge-based systems learn patterns from large datasets and are opaque. Almost every system a pharmacist meets today is knowledge-based, and the pharmacist's work is writing and validating the rules.

Session 6.1 · Ordering

Order conditions by descending risk

Only the first matching branch of an if-else structure executes. If a moderate threshold is tested before a critical one, a patient meeting both is handled as moderate — and the rule creates the failure it was written to prevent. This is the commonest defect in student-written logic.

Session 6.2 · Structure

Three categories, only one of which executes

Maintenance is the identity card, library is the evidence, and knowledge is the executable core. The computer reads only the third. The first two are what let a pharmacist defend an alert instead of shrugging at a black box.

Session 6.2 · The five slots

Data, evoke, logic, action, urgency

Data fetches what the rule needs. Evoke decides when it runs. Logic asks one question and answers true or false. Action says what happens. Urgency says how loudly, on a scale from one to ninety-nine.

The fifth statement

Silence is the default. A rule that concludes false produces no record that it ran at all, which is why a rule reading an empty field behaves exactly like a rule that was never installed. That is the same failure you met in Weeks 3, 4 and 5, seen now from inside the rule.

Where the marks are

Now interrogate the rule

With the slots in place, ask three questions of it. What happens to a patient with an INR of 4.0 exactly — does the rule fire? What happens to a patient whose warfarin was stopped yesterday but whose INR is still 5.2? And when does this rule run for a patient whose INR was measured before the warfarin was prescribed? Each answer is determined by a single word in a single slot.

Tier assignment is where most marks are lost. G

Rule-writing workbench

15 min

Two rules to write from a clinical brief. Write your own answer before revealing the model response, and compare the structure as well as the content.

Case 1 · Write a gentamicin renal module
Your hospital asks you to write a rule preventing gentamicin accumulation in renal impairment. The agreed clinical position is that gentamicin is renally cleared, that standard dosing accumulates below a creatinine clearance of 30 mL/min, and that the risk is nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity — the second of which is often irreversible. The evidence base is the current national antimicrobial guideline.
Model response
  • Maintenance. Title: gentamicin renal dose alert. Filename, version 1.0, institution, the named pharmacist author, and today's date as the validation date. None of this affects whether the rule fires; all of it is required for audit and for knowing when the threshold was last checked against guidance.
  • Library. Purpose: prevent gentamicin accumulation and toxicity in patients with a creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min. Explanation: gentamicin is renally eliminated, standard dosing accumulates below this threshold, and the resulting nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity carry a risk of permanent hearing loss. Citations: the current national antimicrobial guideline. This is the paragraph you read aloud when a prescriber challenges the alert.
  • Data. Read the most recent creatinine clearance into a variable, and read the most recent new medication order where the drug is gentamicin into a second variable.
  • Evoke. Fire on the event of a medication order being entered. A scheduled daily trigger would be wrong here: it would warn you the morning after the first dose was given.
  • Logic. Conclude true if the gentamicin order is not null and the clearance is below 30. Both conditions joined by and, so both must hold simultaneously.
  • Action. Write an alert inserting the actual clearance value into the text, stating that dose adjustment or interval extension is required and that levels should be monitored, and naming the person to contact.
  • Urgency. A defensible answer is 85 — a soft stop requiring a written justification to override. The argument for it: the harm is serious and partly irreversible, but it develops over days rather than after the first dose, and a genuine indication for gentamicin in renal impairment exists. An answer of 95 is also defensible if argued on the irreversibility of ototoxicity; an answer of 50 is not, because an advisory alert that can be dismissed with one click will be.
  • Marks are lost by omitting the library category, which is where the clinical reasoning lives, and by choosing an urgency without arguing for it. The number alone earns nothing.
Case 2 · Diagnose a rule that never fires
A digoxin toxicity rule has been installed for eight months and has never produced an alert, although the hospital has treated several patients with digoxin and a potassium below 3.0 mmol/L. The rule reads: data — fetch the last potassium and the last active digoxin order; evoke — every 24 hours; logic — conclude true if the digoxin order is not null and potassium is below 3.0; action — write an alert; urgency — 30.
Model response
  • The candidates. Four slots could produce no alert: the data slot fetching a field that is never populated; the evoke slot firing at a time when the condition is not met; the logic slot testing a threshold nothing reaches; or, separately, the alert firing invisibly because of a very low urgency. Only the first three would mean the rule never fired at all.
  • Most likely. The evoke slot. A once-daily schedule checks each patient at one arbitrary moment. A potassium that falls to 2.8 in the afternoon and is corrected by the evening infusion is never in that state when the rule looks. The rule is not wrong; it is asleep at the moment the condition is true.
  • The change, and what it achieves. Trigger on the event of a laboratory result being received, so that the rule evaluates the moment a new potassium arrives, whatever the hour. This is the pattern you saw in Week 5: the result message pushes to whoever needs it rather than waiting for someone to look.
  • The second change. Raise the urgency. At 30 the alert is informational and easily dismissed, which for a genuine digoxin toxicity risk in hypokalaemia is too quiet. A soft stop in the 75 to 89 range is defensible.
  • If it had been firing and ignored. Then the fault is not in evoke but in urgency and in the action text. Look at whether the alert states what to do — check the level, correct the potassium, review the dose — or merely announces that a risk exists. An alert that names no action is dismissed, and dismissal at scale is what produces alert fatigue.
  • Marks are lost by naming only one candidate, and by treating “never fired” and “fired and was ignored” as the same finding. They point at different slots and have different fixes.

Focus your revision here

The five items below account for most of the marks lost on Week 6 material.

  1. Ordering conditions by descending risk. Only the first matching branch runs. A moderate threshold tested before a critical one silently downgrades the sickest patient, and the rule still executes perfectly.
  2. Which slot explains which failure. A rule that never fires points at evoke, data or logic. A rule that fires and is ignored points at urgency and the action text. Read the stem for which of the two is described.
  3. What the computer does not read. Maintenance and library never affect execution, and they are the two categories that keep a rule auditable and defensible. A question asking what a slot is for is usually about these.
  4. Tier assignment on immediacy, not seriousness. Reserve hard stops for harm that can follow the first dose and cannot be undone. Overusing tier 1 destroys the tier.
  5. Knowledge-based against non-knowledge-based. Rules you can read and audit, against patterns learned from data. Your role differs accordingly: write and validate the rules, or interpret and validate the predictions.
